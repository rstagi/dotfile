# Type × time — how the slot reshapes the talk

Type and time do more to determine structure than the topic does. A keynote crammed into a lightning
slot fails; a lecture wearing a "workshop" label fails differently. Budget content to the slot, then
apply the type's tactics. Attributions are short; see
[`../../talk/references/sources.md`](../../talk/references/sources.md).

## The word + idea budget

Baseline: **~130–150 wpm** for content, but **reserve 15–20%** of the clock for pauses, demos,
audience reactions, and breathing. So usable words ≈ `wpm × minutes × 0.8`. Use ~140 wpm as the
working number.

| Slot | Usable words (~140 wpm × 0.8) | Core idea | Supporting beats | Reality check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **5 min** | ~560 | 1 | 0 (maybe 1 example) | One idea, one story, one CTA. No tangents survive. |
| **15 min** | ~1,680 | 1 | 2 | The TED short form. One throughline, two supports, a moment. |
| **30 min** | ~3,360 | 1 | 3–4 | Room to oscillate (sparkline) or chain consequences. |
| **45 min** | ~5,040 | 1 | up to 6 | Risk now is sprawl, not thinness — keep cutting to the throughline. |

The core idea count is always **1**. More time buys more *support* for the one idea, never more
ideas. ~18 minutes (TED) is a deliberate cognitive-load choice (Anderson), not an accident — long
slots are permission to go deeper, not wider.

**Budget the arc against the clock before writing prose.** If the beats don't fit, cut a beat. Never
fix an over-long talk by talking faster — pace is part of the content (see
[`../../talk/references/keep-it-human.md`](../../talk/references/keep-it-human.md) Gate D).

## In-person stage keynote

The default assumptions of every framework. You have the room's full attention, body language to read,
a stage to use. Spend the long slot on depth: one throughline, supports that earn each other, one
designed memorable moment, a close that calls back to the open. Slides are a visual aid, not the
talk — the content brief decides the words; the slide skill decides what's projected.

Tactics: build from where the audience already is (Step 3 map); plant the memorable moment near the
emotional peak; reserve the last 60–90 seconds for the callback + single CTA.

## Online webinar

Same content spine, different attention physics. You can't see the room, and the audience has a
browser tab one click away. Two changes to the structure decided in the brief:

- **Re-hook every 5–8 minutes.** Build an explicit re-engagement beat into the arc at those intervals:
  a question to the chat, a poll, a switch of modality, a "here's where we are" signpost, a story
  restart. Mark these in the outline.
- **Verbal signposting replaces body cues.** Say the structure out loud ("that's the first of three";
  "now the surprising part") because the audience can't read your stance or your stage position.

Keep the throughline and moment identical to the in-person version — only the re-hook scaffolding is
added. (Delivery-side camera/energy tactics belong to `talk-delivery`, not here.)

## Lightning / Ignite

Brutal compression. A lightning talk is **5 minutes, one idea, one CTA** — write it as a single
sparkline beat or a single Story-Spine arc, nothing more. **Ignite** is the strict variant: **20
slides, 15 seconds each, auto-advancing** (5 minutes exactly). For Ignite:

- One beat per slide; the slides drive the clock, so the *words* must hit the slide changes — script
  to the auto-advance, don't fight it.
- Front-load the diagram or image on each slide (no time to read text).
- Save the punch for the final two or three slides — the close lands hardest when the pace has set a
  rhythm.

Don't pour a 15-minute talk into a lightning slot. Pick the single most important beat of the bigger
talk and give *only* that.

## Hands-on workshop = instructional design

A workshop is **not a lecture with exercises bolted on**. It is mostly practice, designed backward
from what participants will be able to *do* at the end. Switch from "narrative" thinking to
"instructional design" thinking. (Bloom; Cathy Moore, *Map It*; Julie Dirksen, *Design for How People
Learn* — sources.md.)

1. **Write measurable objectives — ABCD:**
   - **Audience** — who ("participants will…")
   - **Behavior** — an observable verb from Bloom's taxonomy (build, debug, configure, classify) — not
     "understand" or "be aware of", which can't be observed.
   - **Condition** — given what (a starter repo, a dataset, the docs).
   - **Degree** — to what standard (in 20 min, with a passing test, ≥3 examples).
2. **Design backward from a "you-do" exercise.** The thing they'll do unaided at the end is the
   anchor; everything before it exists to get them there.
3. **Gradual release — I-do / we-do / you-do:** demonstrate it (I-do, short) → do one together
   (we-do) → they do it alone (you-do, the bulk of the time). Repeat per objective.
4. **Budget mostly to practice.** Rule of thumb: lecture/demo ≤ ~30% of the clock; hands-on practice
   ≥ ~60%; ~10% buffer for setup, questions, and running over. If your "workshop" is 80% talking, it's
   a lecture — relabel it or redesign it.

The content brief for a workshop replaces "outline/arc" with **objectives + the I-do/we-do/you-do
sequence per objective + the exercises**. The throughline still holds: it's the one capability the
whole workshop builds toward.

## Quick reference: what changes by type

| | Keynote | Webinar | Lightning/Ignite | Workshop |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core idea count | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 (one capability) |
| Dominant mode | narrative | narrative + signposting | one compressed beat | practice |
| Special structural rule | memorable moment at the peak | re-hook every 5–8 min | one beat/slide, Ignite=20×15s | ABCD objectives, ≥60% practice |
| Biggest failure mode | sprawl in long slots | losing them to the back tab | cramming a bigger talk | lecturing instead of facilitating |
