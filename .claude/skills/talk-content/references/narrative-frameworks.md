# Narrative skeletons — fill-in-the-blank beats

Pick **one** by goal. Don't blend two — a talk that's half sparkline and half Story Circle has no
shape the audience can feel. Fill the chosen skeleton's beats with this talk's content, then sequence
them in the arc. Short attributions below; full bibliography in
[`../../talk/references/sources.md`](../../talk/references/sources.md).

## Pick by goal

| Goal of the talk | Skeleton |
| --- | --- |
| Inspire / shift a belief / move people to act | Duarte sparkline |
| A personal journey or a single transformation | Story Spine (simple) or Harmon Story Circle (deeper) |
| Persuade / pitch / rally toward a new direction | Raskin strategic narrative + Name-the-Enemy |
| Analytical / answer "what should we do" with evidence | SCQA / Minto pyramid |
| Make any of the above *stick* in memory | Heath SUCCESs (overlay, not standalone) |

---

## Duarte sparkline
*Inspirational / change-of-belief talks. Audience = hero, speaker = mentor. Tack repeatedly between
"what is" and "what could be", end on the new bliss + a call to action, and plant one memorable
moment (the STAR — Something They'll Always Remember). — Duarte, Resonate.*

- **Open — what is:** <the audience's status quo, named honestly, so they feel seen>
- **Gap 1 — what could be:** <the first glimpse of the better world your throughline points at>
- **Back to what is:** <the obstacle / why we don't already live there>
- **…oscillate:** <repeat what-is ↔ what-could-be for each main beat; the gap is the engine>
- **The STAR moment:** <the one memorable beat — a demo, a reveal, a number made physical>
- **New bliss:** <the changed world, now concrete and believable because you earned it>
- **Call to action:** <one specific thing the hero does next>

When to use: keynotes meant to move people, advocacy, vision talks. When NOT: a "here are the three
config options" reference talk — there's no gap to oscillate.

## Story Spine (Kenn Adams / Pixar)
*One clean transformation. Adams ~1991, popularized via Emma Coats' Pixar 22 Rules. Best when the
talk IS a story (yours, a customer's, a project's). — Aerogramme Studio writeup.*

- **Once upon a time** <the ordinary world / starting state>
- **Every day** <the stable routine — how things normally went>
- **Until one day** <the inciting incident that broke the routine>
- **Because of that** <the first consequence>
- **Because of that** <the next consequence — chain them>
- **Until finally** <the climax / turning point>
- **And ever since then** <the new normal — which IS your throughline, shown not stated>

When to use: a war story, a postmortem-as-narrative, a founding story. When NOT: a talk with
several parallel ideas — a spine is one thread only.

## Harmon Story Circle
*A deeper transformation arc (the hero changes, not just the situation). Eight beats around a circle.
Use when the talk is about what the journey did to the protagonist. — Reedsy guide.*

1. **You** — a protagonist in a zone of comfort. <who, where>
2. **Need** — they want something. <the desire / the felt lack>
3. **Go** — they cross into an unfamiliar situation. <the threshold>
4. **Search** — they adapt, struggle, try things. <the messy middle>
5. **Find** — they get what they wanted. <the apparent win>
6. **Take** — and pay its price. <the cost / the catch>
7. **Return** — back to the familiar world. <but changed>
8. **Change** — having been transformed. <the new state = the lesson the audience takes>

When to use: personal-growth talks, "what this failure taught me", research-journey talks. When NOT:
short slots — eight beats need ~15+ minutes to breathe.

## Raskin strategic narrative + Name-the-Enemy
*Persuasion / pitch / rally. Five steps. The most under-used and most effective structure for "we
should go this way" talks. — Raskin, "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen" + "Name the Enemy".*

1. **Name a big, undeniable change in the world** <the shift that's already happening, with stakes>
2. **Show there'll be winners and losers** <who thrives if they adapt, who's left behind if they don't>
3. **Tease the Promised Land** <the desirable future — described as a state, not your product>
4. **Introduce the "magic gifts"** <the capabilities that get them there — framed as gifts for the
   journey, evidence each one is real>
5. **Present evidence you can deliver** <proof: results, demo, prior wins>

**Name the Enemy** (do this first, it sharpens all five): the enemy is never a competitor — it's the
**old way of thinking** that the change makes obsolete. <name the obsolete belief>. Everything in the
talk recruits the audience against that enemy.

When to use: internal "let's adopt X", conference talks arguing for a paradigm, fundraising-shaped
talks. When NOT: a neutral educational talk where you have no side.

## SCQA / Minto pyramid
*Analytical, decision-oriented, "answer-first" talks. — Barbara Minto, the Pyramid Principle.*

- **Situation** <the stable, agreed-on context — no one disputes this>
- **Complication** <what changed / the problem / the tension that demands a decision>
- **Question** <the single question the complication raises — the one the audience now has>
- **Answer** <your answer, stated up front (the throughline), then supported>
  - Supporting argument 1 → evidence
  - Supporting argument 2 → evidence
  - Supporting argument 3 → evidence (each argument is itself a mini-pyramid)

When to use: technical decision talks, exec briefings, "here's what the data says we should do".
When NOT: emotional/inspirational talks — answer-first kills the curiosity gap a vision talk needs.

## Heath SUCCESs — the stickiness overlay
*Not a standalone skeleton. Run it as a checklist over whichever skeleton you chose, to make the
beats memorable. — Heath, Made to Stick.*

- **Simple** — is there one core, prioritized idea (the throughline), or are you saying everything?
- **Unexpected** — does a beat break a pattern / open a curiosity gap the audience didn't see coming?
- **Concrete** — sensory, specific, nameable — not "synergy" but "the 40-minute build".
- **Credible** — traced evidence, a testable claim, or a vivid detail that proves you were there.
- **Emotional** — does at least one beat make them *feel* (one identifiable person beats a statistic)?
- **Stories** — is the spine carried by a story, not a list of assertions?

Watch the **Curse of Knowledge** the whole way (Heath): you've forgotten what it's like not to know
this. The fix is concreteness, every time.

## After filling the skeleton

Sequence the beats into the arc (SKILL Step 6), then check: does every beat serve the throughline? Cut
the ones that don't — a beat you love but that doesn't move the one idea is the hardest, most
necessary cut. Run keep-it-human Gate A against the result.
