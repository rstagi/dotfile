# Myths and pitfalls — what not to teach, and what canned delivery sounds like

Two jobs: kill the delivery myths that get repeated as fact, and give the speaker a concrete picture
of what over-coached, recited, "uncanny" delivery sounds like so the recording self-audit can catch
it. Sources in [`../../talk/references/sources.md`](../../talk/references/sources.md).

## Myths — do NOT teach these as fact

### Mehrabian 7-38-55

The claim you'll hear everywhere: "communication is 7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language." It is a
misuse of Albert Mehrabian's research. His experiments measured how people resolve a **conflict
between the words and the tone/face when the message is about feelings or attitudes** (like vs
dislike) — not the information content of an idea, data, or instructional talk. Mehrabian himself has
said: unless a communicator is talking about feelings or attitudes, these equations don't apply.

For a conference talk about an idea, **the words carry most of the meaning.** Tone and body matter a
lot — that's why this whole skill exists — but they support the words; they don't outweigh them
40-to-1. Coach vocal variety and gesture because they *work*, never by citing the 7-38-55 split.
(See `sources.md`; cross-check Wikipedia's Mehrabian entry.)

### Cuddy "power posing"

The claim: standing in a "power pose" for two minutes before a talk raises testosterone, lowers
cortisol, and makes you behave more boldly. The hormonal and behavioral effects **failed to
replicate** (Ranehill et al., n=200, vs the original n=42); co-author Dana Carney later disavowed the
work and described p-hacking in the original; a p-curve analysis found no evidential value; TED
appended a disclaimer to the talk. What survived replication is only the **self-reported feeling** of
being more powerful.

So: a private "stand tall, shoulders back" ritual before you go on is fine — it may help you *feel*
ready, and feeling ready is worth something. Just **don't teach the hormone or behavior claims as
fact**, and don't put "do a power pose to change your testosterone" in the plan. The grounded,
square stance in [`delivery-craft.md`](delivery-craft.md) is good posture coaching on its own merits.

### A note on numbers

Don't invent precise stats to sound authoritative — exact wpm-to-the-decimal, pause-millisecond
counts, "neural coupling" percentages, or fabricated Anderson/Treasure quotes. **Overconfident fake
precision is itself an AI-slop tell.** When a real number exists (gesture counts, the Brooks study,
filler rates), cite it from `sources.md` and mark it as approximate where the source does. When one
doesn't, say "roughly" or leave it out.

## The uncanny-valley / canned-delivery tells

Over-rehearsed, recited, or AI-voiced delivery has a recognizable signature. The paradox: it's
*almost* right, and the brain snags on the missing fraction. Watch (and listen) for:

- **Monotone / flat prosody** — the #1 robot tell. The melody doesn't rise and fall with meaning.
- **Every beat hits a beat too perfectly.** The emphasis is too even, the pacing too regular. Human
  delivery has uneven texture.
- **Invariant breathing; no audible breath.** Real speakers breathe, and you can hear it.
- **Zero self-corrections.** Nobody reaches for a word, nobody restarts a sentence. The absence of
  any micro-hesitation reads as scripted, not as competent.
- **Over-enunciation.** Every consonant pronounced like a diction exam.
- **Flat emotional range** — the "happy" line, the "urgent" line, and the "serious" line all sound
  the same. Emotion should genuinely shift beat to beat.
- **Scripted-prose phrasing leaking into speech** — "At this time…", "Without further ado…", "All in
  all…", "Let me be clear." If it reads like an essay, it sounds like one.
- **No specific personal detail.** Generic, could-be-anyone delivery with no lived, named particular
  is the verbal cousin of low-density slop.

Authentic delivery has *texture*: variable breath, the occasional reach-for-a-word, an emotion that
visibly moves. That's the thing the recording self-audit is hunting for — watch it sound-off first
(sway, adaptors, where stillness lands), then audio-only (monotone, fillers, the seamlessness tell).
The illustrative framing of "almost-human voice → the brain loops on the missing 10%" comes from
marketing/industry writing on AI voiceover, not a controlled study — use it as intuition, not
evidence.

## The watch-these example library — one lens at a time

Pick one or two clips matched to what the speaker most needs, and watch with a single question. Don't
watch six at once trying to absorb everything.

| Watch | For this one lens |
| --- | --- |
| **Bryan Stevenson** — "We need to talk about an injustice" (TED2012) | Conversational authenticity: ~190 wpm, story-driven, no slides, longest TED ovation |
| **Brené Brown** — "The power of vulnerability" | Vulnerability + stillness; comfort with the pause |
| **Sir Ken Robinson** — "Do schools kill creativity?" | Humor woven through, never as bolted-on jokes |
| **Jill Bolte Taylor** — "My stroke of insight" | Landing the emotional beats; the real-brain prop as a memorable moment |
| **Hans Rosling** — any Gapminder talk | Energy + data as performance; honest, vivid numbers |
| **Tim Urban** / **James Veitch** | Humor done right — timing, riding the laugh, self-deprecation |
| **Julian Treasure** — "How to speak…" | The vocal toolbox demonstrated live |

Watch to steal *one move*, not to copy a person. Treasure's own warning applies: the goal is to be
more *yourself*, not a knockoff of a speaker you admire.
