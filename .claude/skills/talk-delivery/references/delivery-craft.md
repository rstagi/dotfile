# Delivery craft — the coaching canon

The substance behind the coaching section of the delivery plan. Every claim here traces to
[`../../talk/references/sources.md`](../../talk/references/sources.md); the myth guardrails live in
[`myths-and-pitfalls.md`](myths-and-pitfalls.md). Pull what the speaker needs, write it in their
voice, and verify any number before you put it in the plan.

## The frame: you are a gift, and you rehearse to own it

Anderson's one line is the whole job: the thing that matters in public speaking is **having
something worth saying** — not confidence, not stage polish, not smooth talking (Chris Anderson,
*TED Talks: The Official TED Guide to Public Speaking*). Frame the talk as a gift you're handing the
room, not a performance you have to nail. That reframe is also the best nerve cure there is, because
it points attention at the audience instead of at yourself.

**Rehearse to own it — Anderson's four modes.** A talk can be delivered:

1. **Scripted + memorized** — fully committed; conversational because it's deeply internalized
   (think Jobs, Bolte Taylor). For an 18-minute talk this is roughly 5–6 hours of rehearsal. Worth
   it for a once-in-a-career keynote; overkill for most.
2. **Scripted + read** — fine for a manuscript at a lectern, deadly on a stage.
3. **Unscripted + structured** — you know the beats cold and find the words live. This is the
   default for most conference talks and what the cue-card notes in this skill are built for.
4. **Off-the-cuff** — only when you genuinely know the material better than anyone in the room.

The danger is the **middle of the memorization zone**: you've half-memorized the words, so you
sound smooth — until one dropped phrase derails the whole thing because you're reciting, not
thinking. Either commit fully (mode 1) or work from trigger cards (mode 3). Never camp in between.
This is why this skill refuses to write a teleprompter script.

## Connection — Anderson's five tools, used early

Connection is deliberate and it happens in the first minute, not when you finally relax. The five
tools: **meet their eyes from the start; show vulnerability; make them laugh; drop your ego; tell a
story.** The practical hack: scan for **3–4 sympathetic faces** spread across the room and give the
talk to them, rotating; hold the far sections a beat longer so the back row feels seen.

## Voice — the instrument (Treasure)

Vocal variety is the instrument; **monotone is the cardinal sin** (and the #1 "robot" tell on a
recording). Julian Treasure's vocal toolbox ("How to speak so that people want to listen", TEDGlobal
2013):

- **Register** — chest voice reads as authority; we vote for and follow deeper, grounded voices.
- **Timbre** — the texture; trainable toward "rich, smooth, warm."
- **Prosody** — the melody and meaning in the rise and fall. Flatten it and the idea dies.
- **Pace** — fast for excitement, slow for weight; vary it (see the pause, below).
- **Pitch** and **volume** — used for meaning, not at a constant max ("sodcasting").

**HAIL** is what to *transmit*: Honesty, Authenticity, Integrity, Love. The "A" — Authenticity — is
the licence to be yourself instead of a highly rehearsed robot. Don't imitate another speaker's
cadence; calibrate to a brisk-conversational baseline (~190 wpm, the Bryan Stevenson register —
story-driven, no slides, the longest standing ovation in TED history) and then *vary* off it.

**The six warm-ups** (Treasure), run ~3 minutes before going on: sigh out loud "aaah"; lip pops
"ba-ba-ba"; lip trill "brrr"; tongue "la-la-la"; rolled R; a pitch siren from low to high and back;
finish with a few deep diaphragmatic breaths. **Vinh Giang** adds three useful drills:
over-articulate a tongue-twister to wake the mouth; the soft-palate / yawn move ("volume for free"
without shouting); and lip trills to loosen the lips.

## The strategic pause

The most underused tool and the cheapest. A pause **before** a key line builds anticipation; a pause
**after** it gives the room time to process. It frames the idea, signals "this matters," and —
critically — it is the substitute for a filler word. Zandan calls the pause a "rhetorical Nicorette."
Never step on a laugh with the next sentence; let it breathe. Mark `[PAUSE]` on the throughline, the
memorable moment, the turn of each story, and the close.

## Filler substitution (Zandan / Quantified Communications)

Average speakers land around **5 fillers per minute**; the target is roughly **1 per minute**. You
don't eliminate "um" by trying harder mid-sentence — you build *awareness* (record 5+ minutes a day
for ~two weeks until you can hear them coming) and then **substitute a silent diaphragmatic breath**
for the filler. The breath buys the same thinking time without the verbal litter, and it reads as
composure. Mark `[BREATHE]` where a filler habitually sneaks in. (Noah Zandan, HBR 2018.)

## Body and gesture — the power sphere, illustrators vs adaptors

Gestures correlate with engagement: in a study of 2010 TED talks, the **top-rated talks averaged
~465 hand gestures per 18 minutes** (avg 7.3M views) versus **272 for the lowest-rated** (avg 124k
views), across 760 raters — but *quality* is the point, not a gesture quota. (Toastmasters / Vanessa
Van Edwards.)

- **Illustrators** — gestures that come *from the thought* and illustrate it — help.
- **Adaptors** — self-soothing fidgets: face-touching, hair, sleeve-pulling, the "microphone bob,"
  rhythmic swaying — hurt. Film yourself sound-off and hunt them down.
- The **power sphere** (Gallo) is roughly eyes-to-navel; keep illustrative gestures there. Stand
  grounded: feet shoulder-width, weight on the arches, hips and shoulders square, chin level. On the
  single most important line, **plant and hold** still for 2–4 seconds — stillness is emphasis.

**Presence / the Second Circle** (Patsy Rodenburg, *The Second Circle: How to Use Positive Energy
for Success in Every Situation*): aim for Second Circle — fully present, energy flowing *to and from*
the audience — rather than First Circle (withdrawn, energy pulled inward) or Third Circle (forcing,
performing *at* the room). Note: the book titled *Presence: Bring Your Boldest Self…* is Amy Cuddy's,
not Rodenburg's — don't conflate them.

## Nerves — reframe, then breathe

Trying to "calm down" fights your own physiology. The better move is **reappraisal**: say "I am
excited." Same arousal, reframed as opportunity, and measurably more persuasive and confident in
performance (Alison Wood Brooks, "Get Excited," *J. Experimental Psychology: General* 2014,
143(3):1144–1158). Pair it with breathing — box breathing (4-4-4-4) or 4-7-8 — and **burn the
adrenaline physically** beforehand (a brisk walk, shake out the hands). The body settles within about
90 seconds once you start; the first minute is the worst minute, and the opening line being
hand-written and over-rehearsed is what carries you through it. Last-resort recovery: if something
breaks, you can tell the audience you're nervous — they want to root for you. Have a recovery line
ready for a flub or an AV death.

## Humor — found, not manufactured

Humor is high-reward and high-risk. Popular TED talks used humor far more than unpopular ones (~12.9
instances vs ~3.9 in one analysis — Shoda & Yamanaka, *Behavioral Sciences* 2022, PMC8772906), but
**ineffective humor is worse than none.** The rules:

- **Find it in the material; don't write jokes.** The funniest moments are the true, specific,
  slightly absurd details already in your story. You're not a stand-up.
- **Self-deprecate; never mock the audience.** Cleese's point: if I get you to laugh *with* me, you
  like me more.
- **Relevant, and culturally safe.** Humor rarely travels across cultures; skip sarcasm and off-color
  material in front of a mixed room.
- **Ride the laugh.** Pause and let it land; if it flops, move on without apologizing.

Run the humor check in the plan honestly: if there's no genuine funny beat, "none" is a fine answer.
