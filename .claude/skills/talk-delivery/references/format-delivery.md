# Format delivery — stage vs online vs workshop

Pick **one** primary context for the plan and write its tactics. The three are different
instruments; what helps on a stage can sink you on camera, and a workshop is a different job
entirely. Claims trace to [`../../talk/references/sources.md`](../../talk/references/sources.md).

---

## In-person stage

You have a body, a room, and a back row. Use them.

- **Claim the space.** Walk on, plant, take a beat of silence, then the opening line. Don't start
  talking while still walking — the first words get thrown away.
- **Move with purpose, then be still.** Move to mark a transition (a new beat, a new place in the
  story); stand still for the line that matters. Pacing-as-nerves reads as nerves; movement that
  maps to structure reads as command. Kill the unconscious sway.
- **Eye contact across the room.** Find 3–4 friendly faces spread left-to-right and rotate; hold the
  far sections slightly longer so the back row feels addressed, not the front row.
- **Project to the back wall, not the front row.** Soft-palate / yawn warm-up gives you "volume for
  free" without shouting (Vinh Giang).
- **Gestures in the power sphere**, illustrative, from thought; adaptors eliminated (see
  [`delivery-craft.md`](delivery-craft.md)).
- **Mind the lectern and the clicker.** If there's a lectern, step out from behind it for the
  emotional beats. Hold the clicker low; don't "microphone-bob" or wave it.
- **Watch the clock without watching the clock.** Know your time checkpoints by beat (e.g. "memorable
  moment by the 12-minute mark") so you can compress or expand the un-load-bearing middle live.

Recording self-audit emphasis: sound-OFF for sway/adaptors and whether stillness lands on the key
line.

## Online

Video flattens energy — a useful heuristic is that you lose roughly a third of your expressiveness
through the lens — so you compensate, and the failure modes are all about the camera.

- **Dial energy up ~⅓.** Bigger gestures (make them ~30% larger than you would on a stage), more
  pitch range, a touch more pace variation. What feels slightly "too much" in the room reads as
  *normal* on screen.
- **Camera at or slightly above eye level**, lens roughly an arm's length away. Frame your face in
  the upper third, eyes near the top — not a nostril-cam from below, not a face floating dead center.
- **Look at the lens, not the faces.** Looking at the gallery looks like you're looking *down/away*.
  Hit the lens deliberately at the **opening, each key point, after a question, and the close**; the
  rest of the time the notes are fine.
- **Self-view OFF.** Watching your own feed produces shifty downward gaze and self-consciousness.
  Cover it or hide the tile.
- **Stand up if you can.** Standing opens the diaphragm and brings energy that sitting kills.
- **Light the face, never backlit.** A window or a lamp in front of you; no bright window behind.
- **Start camera-on, slides-off.** Let them see a person for the first beat before you share the
  deck; come back to face-only for the emotional beats and the close.
- **Re-hook every ~10 minutes.** No body-language feedback loop means attention drifts silently —
  build in explicit attention resets (a direct question, a poll, a "look at this," a hard cut to a
  new visual) roughly every 10 minutes.
- **Verbal signposting.** You've lost the room's body cues, so say the structure out loud: "that's
  the first of three; here's the second."

Recording self-audit emphasis: audio-only for monotone (flat prosody is worse on a small speaker)
and whether the lens-contact moments actually connect.

## Workshop facilitation

A workshop is not a talk with exercises bolted on — your job flips from performer to facilitator. If
the session is majority lecture, it's a talk, and you should have routed back to `talk-content` to
reshape it.

- **Be the guide, not the hero.** The participants do the work and have the insight; you set it up,
  hold the space, and get out of the way. Talking time should be the minority of the clock.
- **Time-box every activity with exact instructions.** "You have 12 minutes. Three ideas, one per
  sticky note. Go." Ambiguous instructions burn the activity. Give a warning ("3 minutes left").
- **Build in ~10% buffer and finish early.** Activities always run long; a workshop that runs over is
  remembered as a workshop that ran over.
- **Note & Vote / silent-then-share.** Have people generate silently first (everyone contributes,
  not just the loudest), then share or dot-vote. It beats open brainstorm, which the extroverts win.
- **Count to 7 after a question.** Silence feels eternal to the facilitator and normal to the room.
  Don't rescue it — the answer comes if you wait.
- **Manage the dominator and the parking lot.** Gently redirect the one person who's monopolizing;
  put off-topic-but-valuable threads in a visible "parking lot" so you can move on without dismissing
  them.
- **Energy management.** Watch for the post-lunch dip; a 90-second stand-and-stretch resets it. Keep
  the gradual-release shape: I-do (you demo) / we-do (together) / you-do (they practice), with most
  time on the last.

Recording self-audit emphasis: did you stay the guide (talk-time ratio), did every activity get its
full time-box, did you finish on time?

## Quick chooser

| Context | The compensating move | The #1 failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In-person stage | move-with-purpose then be still | unconscious sway / pacing-as-nerves |
| Online | +⅓ energy, look at the lens, self-view off | watching your own feed; nostril-cam; backlit |
| Workshop | time-box + Note&Vote + count to 7 | being the hero; running over; open brainstorm |
